# Tennyson's Swallow Song

The broken appeal to a swallow near the end recalls a song in Alfred Tennyson's *The Princess*, Part IV. A lover asks the migrating bird to fly south and carry messages to an absent woman. The [song in Tennyson's poem](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/791/791-h/791-h.htm) joins seasonal movement to longing and the wish that a message might reach its destination.

Eliot gives only the opening call, placing it beside the Latin swallow of the *Pervigilium Veneris*. Marie's earlier winter journey south can also be heard against this migratory pattern, though that connection is less explicit. Near the close, literary birds and human travel become fragments of a shared desire for return and renewed speech.

O Swallow, Swallow
English source: Alfred, Lord Tennyson

O Swallow, Swallow, flying, flying South,
Fly to her, and fall upon her gilded eaves,
And tell her, tell her, what I tell to thee.

O tell her, Swallow, thou that knowest each,
That bright and fierce and fickle is the South,
And dark and true and tender is the North.

O Swallow, Swallow, if I could follow, and light
Upon her lattice, I would pipe and trill,
And cheep and twitter twenty million loves.

O were I thou that she might take me in,
And lay me on her bosom, and her heart
Would rock the snowy cradle till I died.

Why lingereth she to clothe her heart with love,
Delaying as the tender ash delays
To clothe herself, when all the woods are green?

O tell her, Swallow, that thy brood is flown:
Say to her, I do but wanton in the South,
But in the North long since my nest is made.

O tell her, brief is life but love is long,
And brief the sun of summer in the North,
And brief the moon of beauty in the South.

O Swallow, flying from the golden woods,
Fly to her, and pipe and woo her, and make her mine,
And tell her, tell her, that I follow thee.
