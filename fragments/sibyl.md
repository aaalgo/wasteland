# The Cumaean Sibyl

The epigraph comes from Petronius's *Satyricon*, section 48. Trimalchio claims to have seen the Sibyl at Cumae suspended in a small vessel. When boys ask in Greek what she wants, she replies that she wants to die. The Latin narration encloses her Greek answer, making the poem's first voice already a fragment carried across languages.

The background to her aging appears in [Ovid's *Metamorphoses*, Book XIV, lines 130–153](https://www.poetryintranslation.com/PITBR/Latin/Metamorph14.php). Apollo offered to grant her a wish. She asked for as many years of life as there were grains in a heap of dust, but forgot to ask that those years also preserve her youth. Apollo offered youth too if she would yield to him; she refused. When she tells Aeneas her story, she has already lived seven hundred years and must endure another three hundred. She foresees her body dwindling under the weight of age until only her voice remains. This is prolonged mortal life without relief from aging, giving a background to the exhausted figure in Petronius's vessel.

A prophet traditionally associated with knowledge of the future appears here diminished and trapped. The poem begins with life persisting beyond desire, rather than death simply opposing life. Her exhausted wish shadows the later fortune-teller and the many speakers who occupy uncertain states between living and dead.

Read through her story, “fear in a handful of dust” carries the fear of time and aging: each grain of dust or sand becomes another year to be endured. The handful that once promised abundant life measures the slow exhaustion of the body. In this reading, what causes fear is time's power to keep aging us while life continues, until the Sibyl can desire only its end. This is an interpretive connection between the epigraph and the later passage, rather than an explicit source identification supplied by Eliot.

## Prophecy as scattered fragments

The Sibyl's way of giving prophecies offers a clue to the form of *The Waste Land*. In [Virgil's *Aeneid*, Book III, lines 441–460](https://dcc.dickinson.edu/vergil-aeneid/vergil-aeneid-iii-441-462), Helenus describes her as an inspired prophetess who sings the fates and writes signs and names on leaves. She arranges her prophetic verses in order inside her cave. But when the door opens, a breeze can scatter the leaves. She does not gather them again, restore their positions, or rejoin the verses. Visitors leave without the guidance they sought. Helenus therefore advises Aeneas to ask her to speak the prophecy herself.

The revelation survives in pieces, but the connections that make it intelligible can be lost. This gives the opening Sibyl a further significance: she can be read as Eliot's hint about how to approach the poem that follows. Its changes of language, interrupted voices, quotations, and displaced memories resemble leaves from a scattered oracle. The reader must listen across the breaks, notice returning words and images, and bring separated passages into relation. A fragment may become clearer through another fragment far away, or through the older work from which it has been torn.

On this reading, the poem asks us to gather the scattered leaves of a shared consciousness. That gathering need not produce a single continuous story or a final, unambiguous prediction: damaged connections and competing voices are part of the experience. The Sibyl's leaves belong to Virgil's account, not to the Petronius passage quoted in the epigraph; their use here as an invitation to read by assembling fragments is an interpretation of Eliot's choice of opening figure, rather than an explicit instruction from him.

## The epigraph, word by word

> Nam Sibyllam quidem Cumis ego ipse oculis meis vidi in ampulla pendere, et cum illi pueri dicerent: Σίβυλλα τί θέλεις; respondebat illa: ἀποθανεῖν θέλω.

Translation: “For I myself actually saw the Sibyl at Cumae with my own eyes, hanging in a little bottle; and when the boys asked her, ‘Sibyl, what do you want?’, she would answer, ‘I want to die.’”

The narration is Latin; the question and answer are Greek. The text is from [Petronius, *Satyricon* 48.8 (PHI Latin Texts)](https://latin.packhum.org/loc/972/1/28/863-867). The tables follow the spelling of the poem's epigraph; dictionary forms appear separately. “Singular” and “plural” describe grammatical number; a verb's person identifies its subject: first person “I,” second person “you,” third person “he/she/it” or “they.”

### Latin narration

Nam
  Dictionary form: nam
  Parse: Conjunction
  Explanation: “For”: connects Trimalchio's claim to his preceding talk in Petronius; Eliot preserves this connective even though that context is absent.

Sibyllam
  Dictionary form: Sibylla
  Parse: Accusative feminine singular noun
  Explanation: “The Sibyl”: object of vidi and the understood subject of pendere—the person seen hanging.

quidem
  Dictionary form: quidem
  Parse: Adverb / emphasizing particle
  Explanation: “Indeed,” “actually”: reinforces his claim to have seen her.

Cumis
  Dictionary form: Cumae
  Parse: Locative plural place-name
  Explanation: “At Cumae.” The town's Latin name is plural; the locative ending coincides with the dative and ablative plural.

ego
  Dictionary form: ego
  Parse: Nominative first-person singular pronoun
  Explanation: “I”: explicitly states the subject already indicated by vidi.

ipse
  Dictionary form: ipse
  Parse: Nominative masculine singular intensive pronoun
  Explanation: “Myself”: agrees with ego, the male speaker Trimalchio, and emphasizes personal witness.

oculis
  Dictionary form: oculus
  Parse: Ablative masculine plural noun
  Explanation: “With [my] eyes”: ablative of means, explaining how he saw her.

meis
  Dictionary form: meus
  Parse: Ablative masculine plural possessive adjective
  Explanation: “My own”: agrees with oculis.

vidi
  Dictionary form: video
  Parse: First-person singular perfect active indicative
  Explanation: “I saw” / “I have seen”: the main verb.

in
  Dictionary form: in
  Parse: Preposition governing the ablative here
  Explanation: “In”: marks location, rather than motion into something.

ampulla
  Dictionary form: ampulla
  Parse: Ablative feminine singular noun
  Explanation: “A little bottle” or “flask”: object of in, the vessel in which she hangs.

pendere
  Dictionary form: pendeo
  Parse: Present active infinitive
  Explanation: “To hang,” here “hanging”: complements vidi, with Sibyllam as its accusative subject. Its present tense makes the hanging simultaneous with the seeing.

et
  Dictionary form: et
  Parse: Coordinating conjunction
  Explanation: “And”: joins the account of seeing to the exchange that follows.

cum
  Dictionary form: cum
  Parse: Temporal subordinating conjunction
  Explanation: “When”: introduces a past circumstance with the subjunctive dicerent.

illi
  Dictionary form: ille
  Parse: Dative feminine singular demonstrative pronoun
  Explanation: “To her”: refers to the Sibyl, the person addressed. The form could elsewhere be nominative masculine plural (“those”), but here it supplies the recipient of dicerent.

pueri
  Dictionary form: puer
  Parse: Nominative masculine plural noun
  Explanation: “Boys”: subject of dicerent.

dicerent
  Dictionary form: dico
  Parse: Third-person plural imperfect active subjunctive
  Explanation: “Were saying,” here “asked”: the verb in the temporal cum clause, introducing their Greek words.

respondebat
  Dictionary form: respondeo
  Parse: Third-person singular imperfect active indicative
  Explanation: “She was answering” / “she would answer”: the imperfect presents an ongoing or repeated response.

illa
  Dictionary form: ille
  Parse: Nominative feminine singular demonstrative pronoun
  Explanation: “She”: explicitly names the Sibyl as the subject of respondebat.

### Greek question and answer

Σίβυλλα
  Dictionary form: Σίβυλλα
  Parse: Vocative feminine singular noun
  Explanation: “Sibyl”: direct address; its form is identical to the nominative.

τί
  Dictionary form: τίς / τί
  Parse: Accusative neuter singular interrogative pronoun
  Explanation: “What?”: object of θέλεις. The Greek semicolon-shaped mark after θέλεις is a question mark.

θέλεις
  Dictionary form: θέλω
  Parse: Second-person singular present active indicative
  Explanation: “You want”: together, τί θέλεις; means “What do you want?”

ἀποθανεῖν
  Dictionary form: ἀποθνῄσκω
  Parse: Second-aorist active infinitive
  Explanation: “To die”: complements θέλω. The aorist presents dying as an event as a whole; it does not mean past time in this construction.

θέλω
  Dictionary form: θέλω
  Parse: First-person singular present active indicative
  Explanation: “I want”: the subject “I” is contained in the verb ending; ἀποθανεῖν θέλω means “I want to die.”

The sentence turns on two infinitives: Trimalchio sees the Sibyl *pendere*, “hanging,” while she wants *ἀποθανεῖν*, “to die.” The emphatic *ego ipse oculis meis* (“I myself, with my own eyes”) insists on the witness's presence; the final Greek answer reduces the Sibyl's desire to two words.
