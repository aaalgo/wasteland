# Chaucer and the Canterbury Tales

Eliot's opening recalls the first eighteen lines of the General Prologue
to Geoffrey Chaucer's *The Canterbury Tales*. Chaucer's April rain brings
flowers, birdsong, and the desire to go on pilgrimage. Eliot makes this
renewal painful: spring stirs memory and desire in the apparently dead
earth, while winter offers the comfort of forgetfulness.

## Middle English

Chaucer wrote in Middle English, rather than Old English. The passage
below follows [the University of Virginia's edition](https://anthology.lib.virginia.edu/work/Chaucer/chaucer-prologue),
based on Walter Skeat's 1900 text.

> Whan that Aprille with his shoures sote  
> The droghte of Marche hath perced to the rote,  
> And bathed every veyne in swich licour,  
> Of which vertu engendred is the flour;  
> Whan Zephirus eek with his swete breeth  
> Inspired hath in every holt and heeth  
> The tendre croppes, and the yonge sonne  
> Hath in the Ram his halfe cours y-ronne,  
> And smale fowles maken melodye,  
> That slepen al the night with open yë,  
> (So priketh hem nature in hir corages):  
> Than longen folk to goon on pilgrimages  
> (And palmers for to seken straunge strondes)  
> To ferne halwes, couthe in sondry londes;  
> And specially, from every shires ende  
> Of Engelond, to Caunterbury they wende,  
> The holy blisful martir for to seke,  
> That hem hath holpen, whan that they were seke.

## Modern English

A fresh, literal translation of the passage above:

> When April with its sweet showers  
> Has pierced March's drought down to the root,  
> And bathed every vein in such liquid  
> Whose power brings the flower into being;  
> When the west wind, too, with its sweet breath  
> Has breathed life, in every wood and heath,  
> Into the tender shoots, and the young sun  
> Has run half its course through Aries,  
> And little birds make melody,  
> Sleeping all night with open eyes  
> (So nature spurs them in their hearts):  
> Then people long to go on pilgrimages  
> (And pilgrims to seek out foreign shores),  
> To distant shrines, known in various lands;  
> And especially, from every corner  
> Of England, they travel to Canterbury,  
> To seek the holy, blessed martyr  
> Who helped them when they were sick.

The martyr is Thomas Becket, whose shrine at Canterbury is the pilgrims'
destination. “Zephirus” is the west wind; “the Ram” is the zodiac sign
Aries. The opening moves from the awakening of plants and birds to a
human journey toward healing—a movement Eliot's opening unsettles.
