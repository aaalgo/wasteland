# Marie von Moritz and Countess Marie Larisch

The speaker called Marie remembers the Starnbergersee, Munich's Hofgarten, and a childhood sled ride with an aristocratic cousin. She is often associated with Countess Marie Larisch, but Eliot's own account identifies Marie von Moritz as the source of the remembered conversation. The German declaration of Lithuanian origin and German identity belongs first to this particular remembered voice.

In his [letter to Emily Hale of 2 March 1931](https://tseliot.com/the-eliot-hale-letters/letters/l21), Eliot describes von Moritz as a woman of about his own age at the time of writing, who had stayed in the same Munich boarding house as him and sometimes accompanied him on walks. He says that her conversation appears in the poem “almost word for word.” The letter's editorial note also records his later marginal annotation identifying her as a Balt who claimed the Duke of Liechtenstein as a cousin. This gives the Baltic declaration and aristocratic recollections a source in an actual encounter, while leaving the poem's transformation of that conversation open to interpretation.

Countess Marie Larisch remains a parallel association in the passage's reception: the older identification connects Marie's aristocratic memories with the vanished world of the Habsburg court and Larisch's memoir, *My Past* (1913). That association should be distinguished from von Moritz's documented role as the source of the conversation. [Frances Dickey's report for the International T. S. Eliot Society](https://tseliotsociety.wildapricot.org/news/8493527), published when the Hale letters became available in January 2020, brought Eliot's identification of von Moritz to renewed attention.

Her recollections offer the surface of a privileged European past: travel, coffee, family connections, and the freedom of the mountains. Abrupt transitions make that world feel accessible only in scraps. The fear of the sled ride survives more vividly than a connected life story, and the movement between countries unsettles any simple idea of home.

Bin gar keine Russin, stamm’ aus Litauen, echt deutsch.

Bin: “am,” from sein (“to be”). The subject ich (“I”) is understood but omitted.
gar: “at all,” strengthening the denial in gar keine.
keine: “no” or “not a,” agreeing with the feminine noun Russin.
Russin: “Russian woman.” Together, Bin gar keine Russin means “I am not Russian at all.”
stamm’: shortened form of stamme, from stammen (“to come from” or “to originate”). Again, ich is understood.
aus: “from.”
Litauen: “Lithuania.” Stamm’ aus Litauen means “I come from Lithuania.”
echt: “genuinely” or “truly.”
deutsch: “German.” Echt deutsch means “genuinely German,” asserting German identity despite the speaker’s Lithuanian origin.

The whole line means: “I am not Russian at all; I come from Lithuania, genuinely German.”

Pronunciation (standard German; capitals indicate stressed syllables):

Bin: bin, with the short i of English “sit.”
gar: gahr, with the long a of “father.”
keine: KY-nuh; “ky” rhymes with “eye.”
Russin: ROOS-in; keep the oo short, as in “foot,” not “food.”
stamm’: shtahm, with a short a and no extra syllable after the m.
aus: owss; “ow” as in “cow,” followed by an s sound.
Litauen: lee-TOW-un; “tow” rhymes with “cow.”
echt: ekht; kh represents the soft German ich sound, made with the tongue near the hard palate, like the h at the start of a strongly whispered “huge.” It is not a k sound.
deutsch: doytch; “oy” as in “boy,” ending with the ch of “church.”

The whole line, approximately: bin gahr KY-nuh ROOS-in, shtahm owss lee-TOW-un, ekht doytch.
