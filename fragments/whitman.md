# Walt Whitman, Lilacs, and the Hermit Thrush

The lilacs invite comparison with Walt Whitman's elegy *When Lilacs Last in the Dooryard Bloom'd*. Whitman's poem joins returning spring, the mourning of Lincoln, and a hermit thrush whose song helps the speaker confront death. The [poem itself](https://www.poetryfoundation.org/poems/45480/when-lilacs-last-in-the-dooryard-bloomd) develops these images across an extended act of remembrance.

A more direct correspondence appears in the hermit-thrush passage at lines 357–358, within the earlier evocation of a landscape without water:

> Here is no water but only rock
> […]
> Where the hermit-thrush sings in the pine trees
> Drip drop drip drop drop drop drop
> But there is no water

The apparent dripping comes from the hermit thrush's song. Eliot's own note to line 357 specifically celebrates its “water-dripping song.” He recalls hearing the bird in Quebec County and cites Frank Chapman's *Handbook of Birds of Eastern North America* on its secluded woodland habitat and the purity and sweetness of its tone. In the dry mountain landscape, the song briefly makes an imagined spring audible, before the blunt reminder that there is no water. It awakens the sensation of relief without supplying the water the exhausted travellers need.

The hermit thrush is also a central image in Whitman's elegy:

> Solitary the thrush,
> The hermit withdrawn to himself, avoiding the settlements,
> Sings by himself a song.

Whitman ultimately brings the lilac, the star, and the bird's song together in remembrance of the dead. Eliot's lilacs emerge painfully from dead land, while his later thrush seems to sing water into a landscape that has none. The connection to Whitman remains an interpretive echo, rather than an attribution Eliot makes in his notes. Where Whitman gathers these images into a sustained mourning song, Eliot lets them return as scattered, troubled reminders of the desire for consolation.
