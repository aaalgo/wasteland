# Spenser's Prothalamion

Eliot's note to line 176 names Edmund Spenser's *Prothalamion* (1596), a song celebrating a double marriage. Its refrain closes each of its ten stanzas. The opening riverbank supplies the landscape that “The Fire Sermon” remembers and empties:

> Along the shoare of siluer streaming Themmes,  
> Whose rutty Bancke, the which his Riuer hemmes,  
> Was paynted all with variable flowers,  
> And all the meades adornd with daintie gemmes,  
> Fit to decke maydens bowres,  
> And crowne their Paramours,  
> Against the Brydale day, which is not long:  
> Sweete Themmes runne softly, till I end my Song.

— Stanza 1, lines 11–18.

The next stanza introduces the figures whose departure Eliot announces:

> There, in a Meadow, by the Riuers side,  
> A Flocke of Nymphes I chaunced to espy,  
> All louely Daughters of the Flood thereby,  
> With goodly greenish locks all loose vntyde,  
> As each had bene a Bryde;

— Stanza 2, lines 19–23.

Later, the nymphs scatter flowers over the two bridal swans and the water:

> Then forth they all out of their baskets drew,  
> Great store of Flowers, the honour of the field,  
> That to the sense did fragrant odours yeild,  
> All which vpon those goodly Birds they threw,  
> And all the Waues did strew,

— Stanza 5, lines 73–77.

The quotations retain the old spelling of the [Spenser Archive text](https://talus.artsci.wustl.edu/spenserArchivePrototype/html/Prothalamion_poems.html); “Themmes” is the Thames, and “rutty” means root-filled.

In Eliot, the flower-strewn wedding river becomes a bank haunted by the absent litter of summer encounters. “The nymphs are departed” removes both Spenser's river spirits and the modern lovers who might take their place. The refrain survives, but Eliot's added “for I speak not loud or long” makes its public celebration sound exhausted and private. A shared song persists after its company has gone.

Spenser's celebration already carries an undertone of disappointment: his speaker begins by seeking relief from frustrated hopes at court. Eliot draws out the grief within the remembered ceremonial song rather than simply overturning an innocent pastoral scene.
