# The Hyacinth Girl

The hyacinth girl recalls a gift of flowers and a return from a garden. Her remembered speech meets another voice's account of failed speech and sight. The encounter reaches an intensity that the speaker can describe only by suspending the ordinary categories of life, death, and knowledge.

Hyacinths suggest spring and the possibility of renewal, already made painful by the poem's opening. The classical story of Hyacinthus, the beautiful youth whose death produces a flower, offers a further resonance, but the passage does not explicitly name him. Bracketed by quotations from Wagner's *Tristan und Isolde*, this private memory belongs to a larger pattern of desire, separation, and an answer that never arrives.
